Where does America search for health?

Fourteen years of Google search interest in nine health conditions across every US metro area — where concern is concentrated, which worries travel together, and how the nation's leading causes of death changed over the same period.

    What the data shows

    Calculated live from the dataset in your browser.

      How to read these numbers. Google Trends scores are not search counts. For each condition and each year separately, the metro area with the highest share of searches on that topic scores 100 and every other metro is scaled relative to it. Scores therefore compare places — they can't say whether cancer is searched more than diabetes, or whether searches grew over time.

      CDC

      How US death rates changed, 2004–2017

      Age-adjusted deaths per 100,000 people for the ten leading causes (log scale, so changes in smaller causes stay visible).

      The big picture: heart disease and cancer — still the two leading killers — fell steadily, while unintentional injuries (largely drug overdoses), Alzheimer's disease and suicide rose. The CDC table is national, so it can't be matched to metro-level search interest directly; see the assistant's answer to "Do searches match death rates?"

      Data quality

      Corrections made in this version

      An audit of the original database found errors that affected the map and the charts. All fixes are scripted in tools/clean_data.py.

      Wrong statesFixed

      18 metro areas that span two or more states were filed under the last state in their name — e.g. "Tallahassee FL-Thomasville GA" under Georgia — so Washington DC, Rhode Island and Vermont were missing. Each is now assigned to the state it's named after.

      Wrong coordinatesFixed

      23 metros fell outside their own state after geocoding by bare city name: Victoria TX was placed in Australia, Lima OH in Peru, Albany GA in New York. All 210 now sit inside their state.

      CDC typoFixed

      The 2017 cancer death rate read 52.5 instead of the CDC's 152.5 per 100,000, which showed cancer deaths falling by two-thirds in a single year.

      Invalid comparisonsRemoved

      The original dashboard summed scores to claim "cancer is the most searched condition" and "+300% growth". Because each condition and year is scaled separately, those comparisons aren't meaningful and have been replaced.

      How it was done

      Methodology

      1. Collect

        Downloaded Google Trends regional interest for nine health topics in 210 Nielsen DMA metro areas, one value per year, plus CDC leading-causes-of-death tables.

      2. Clean

        Reshaped the wide Trends export with Pandas, mapped each DMA to its state and coordinates, and audited the result.

      3. Store

        Loaded the tables into PostgreSQL. This version pre-builds them as static JSON, so the site needs no server and never goes down.

      4. Analyse

        Compared places within each condition, correlated conditions across metros (Pearson r) and charted CDC trends — computed live in the browser.
